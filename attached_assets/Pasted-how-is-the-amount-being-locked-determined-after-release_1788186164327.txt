The 1 billion XRP monthly unlock is predetermined by escrow contracts created in 2017—not recalculated based on price, demand, or market conditions. What is discretionary is how much Ripple subsequently places into new escrow locks.

How the release works
Ripple originally placed 55 billion XRP into 55 cryptographic escrow contracts, each designed to release up to 1 billion XRP on a monthly schedule. On the first day of a month, that month’s contract expires and the XRP becomes available to Ripple.

That means the initial release amount is fixed:

How the relock amount is determined
After the unlock, Ripple decides how much XRP it needs or intends to use—potentially for institutional sales, liquidity programs, ecosystem funding, acquisitions, operational needs, or other corporate purposes. It then generally puts the unused remainder into new time-locked escrows.

Is there a published formula?
No. Ripple does not appear to publish a binding public formula such as “relock 70% every month” or “relock based on trading volume.” The amount is determined by Ripple’s management and treasury decisions at the time.

Historically, however, the pattern has been fairly consistent:

Step	Typical amount
Scheduled monthly unlock	1 billion XRP
Common relock range	600–800 million XRP
Net amount left outside escrow	About 200–400 million XRP
For August, the article says Ripple relocked 700 million XRP after the unlock, leaving roughly 300 million XRP outside escrow.

Important nuance
“Outside escrow” is not the same as “sold.” The net amount not relocked becomes available under Ripple’s control, but it could remain in company wallets, be allocated to a program, transferred between wallets, used for private or institutional transactions, or sold over time. The actual market impact therefore depends more on the eventual distribution and sales activity than on the headline 1-billion-XRP unlock itself.

Also, a relocked amount usually enters new escrows with future expiration dates, effectively pushing that XRP’s availability forward rather than permanently removing it. The original escrow balance has declined from 55 billion XRP in 2017 to roughly 38 billion XRP, according to the article.

In plain English
Think of the initial plan as 55 buckets, each holding 1 billion XRP. Every month, Ripple empties one bucket. If it pours only 700 million back into a new bucket at the end of the line, then once it reaches the end of the original 55 buckets, the next bucket contains 700 million—not 1 billion.

CLARITY Act and 20%
Under the current Senate-reported text of H.R. 3633, the relevant mature-blockchain condition includes distributed ownership: no digital-commodity issuer, related person, or affiliated person may beneficially own, in aggregate, 20% or more of the total units. The text also includes a separate condition that at least 50% of units be held by persons other than the issuer, related persons, and affiliated persons.

A few implications are easy to miss:

It is not merely “no one company may own 20%.” The bill’s language aggregates the issuer, related persons, and affiliated persons. It explicitly defines affiliated people broadly enough to include, among others, founders, current/recent executives, directors, and people acquiring at least 5% from the issuer; “related persons” can include certain promoters and people acquiring 1% or more from the issuer.

Escrow does not necessarily solve ownership concentration. XRP in escrow is locked from use until expiry, but it is still associated with the party that created it. The bill focuses on beneficial ownership, not whether coins are liquid this month. A legal analysis would ultimately turn on statutory definitions, ownership structure, control, and future agency interpretation.

This is a proposed framework, not a present legal requirement. The bill’s latest listed status is House-passed and pending Senate action, so neither its 20% threshold nor its classifications are currently operative law.

If Ripple holds roughly 38B XRP, that is approximately 38% of XRP’s 100B maximum supply—well above 20%. That means the business plan could face a strategic fork if this exact standard survives enactment:

Distribute or sell down over time to reduce ownership concentration.

Lock more XRP for longer, which may aid market-supply predictability but would not obviously reduce beneficial ownership.

Seek a legal/structural interpretation under which some holdings do not count in the relevant aggregation—uncertain, and not something an investor should assume.

Rely on a different regulatory route rather than treating the mature-blockchain test as the sole path to usable market infrastructure.

In short, the CLARITY proposal creates an incentive for genuine, broad XRP distribution—but it does not itself create demand capable of absorbing that distribution.

Corporate treasury
Ripple’s XRP treasury is effectively a very large, volatile strategic asset. It provides flexibility: fund operations, make acquisitions, establish partnerships, support liquidity, or deploy capital without conventional equity issuance or debt financing.

That is beneficial only if the XRP is used at a return that exceeds the dilution-like effect of expanding the tradable supply. Put plainly: a 300M-XRP monthly net release can be value-positive if it produces durable payment volume, counterparties, infrastructure, and independent demand; it is value-negative for holders if it primarily finances costs or gets sold into insufficient demand.

ETFs
ETFs create a distinct demand channel. Authorized participants and custodians need XRP to back ETF shares, and sustained net inflows can shift XRP from exchange float into custody. Ripple reports five U.S. spot XRP ETFs and more than 769M XRP in custody as of early March 2026.

However, ETFs do not necessarily require Ripple to distribute XRP directly. ETF issuers and authorized participants can acquire XRP in the market, OTC, or through liquidity providers. A healthy ETF market could therefore:

Deepen regulated access.

Add buy-side demand.

Reduce immediately liquid exchange float.

Encourage better custody, pricing, derivatives, and market-making infrastructure.

But it can also add a market structure in which price is driven more by investment flows than settlement usage. That is not inherently bad, but it should not be confused with proof of payment-utility adoption.
