Yes — adding identity, onboarding, or compliance features to CryptoOwnBank can absolutely move you closer to regulatory crosshairs, because the more you help users get into financial products, prove eligibility, route purchases, or screen access, the more regulators may view you as more than “just software.”

What changes your risk
A plain self-custody interface is generally in a better position than a platform that handles custody, executes transactions for users, or markets access to regulated products. Recent U.S. no-action positions suggest some front-end wallet and interface providers may avoid broker or introducing-broker treatment when they stay tightly scoped and do not take on the services traditionally linked to those regulated roles.

But once you add things like KYC, investor onboarding, eligibility checks, transaction routing, or “approved access” to tokenized offerings, you start looking less like neutral software and more like part of the financial access stack. That raises AML, sanctions, privacy, recordkeeping, and possibly securities or money-transmission questions depending on how the feature works and where users are located.

What is riskiest inside CryptoOwnBank
These features create the most heat:

You collect and store user identity documents — that triggers privacy and data-protection exposure, especially if you operate across jurisdictions. DID systems are often pitched as privacy-preserving specifically because storing personal data centrally increases compliance burden.

You decide who is allowed into an investment, token sale, or regulated product — that can look like broker-dealer, transfer-agent, or placement activity if securities are involved.

You route fiat-to-crypto purchases or monitor transactions — this is where KYC/AML, sanctions, suspicious-activity processes, and travel-rule style expectations start becoming very real.

You market it as a compliance-safe gateway — the more you represent yourself as the compliance layer, the easier it is for regulators to say you are performing a regulated function rather than merely offering tools.

Separate brand vs same brand
From a legal-risk and brand-containment standpoint, there is a strong argument for doing this on a separate site/app/entity if the new product touches KYC, investor onboarding, credential issuance, or access control for regulated products.

That separation helps because:

It keeps CryptoOwnBank cleaner as the non-custodial control layer you’ve been aiming for.

It lets you design the new product with its own terms, privacy policy, vendor stack, recordkeeping, and geo-restrictions from day one.

If regulators question the onboarding/compliance business, they are not automatically looking at every feature and every user promise made on CryptoOwnBank under the same umbrella.

That said, separate branding does not magically remove regulation. If the underlying activity is regulated, regulators can still look through the structure to what you actually do.

Best structure in your situation
The lowest-friction path is probably:

Keep CryptoOwnBank focused on self-custody, dashboards, alerts, routing visibility, education, and user-controlled workflows. That is closer to the safer “tool/interface” zone reflected in recent U.S. relief for certain front-end providers.

Put anything involving identity, onboarding, reusable KYC credentials, investor suitability, or permissioned access into a separate product and possibly separate entity.

Make the boundary explicit: CryptoOwnBank does not verify identity, decide eligibility, take custody, or intermediate regulated offerings; the separate product handles those workflows with its own compliance architecture.

Practical rule
A simple test is this: if the feature answers “Who is this person, are they allowed, and can they transact?”, it should probably live outside CryptoOwnBank.

If the feature answers “What do you own, where is it, what’s the risk, and how do you control it yourself?”, it fits CryptoOwnBank much better.

My read: identity wallet, investor onboarding, and compliance rails are promising opportunities, but they are the exact kind of additions that can muddy your non-custodial posture. A separate app/entity is the cleaner move unless you deliberately want CryptoOwnBank to evolve into a regulated-access platform.
