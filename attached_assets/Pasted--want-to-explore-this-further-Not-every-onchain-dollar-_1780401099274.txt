They’re related, but not the same horse with a different name. The big dividing lines are who owes you the money, who sets the rules, and where the kill‑switch lives.

Core differences in plain English
Issuer and liability

Stablecoin: A private issuer (company or protocol) promises 1:1 value and holds reserves or runs an algorithm to keep the peg. Your claim is on that issuer’s reserves.

CBDC: The central bank issues it as a direct liability of the state, like digital cash. Your claim is on the central bank itself.

Design goal

Stablecoin: Mostly built for crypto markets—trading, DeFi, cross‑border transfers, dollars on public chains.

CBDC: Built as part of the national payment system—retail payments, financial inclusion, policy transmission, “digital cash” rails.

Control and surveillance

Both can freeze, monitor, or block in practice, but the levers differ. Stablecoin issuers can blacklist addresses or freeze tokens at contract level; CBDCs can embed policy and oversight rules directly in the state’s ledger and access layer.

CBDCs are explicitly designed to sit inside AML/monitoring frameworks; stablecoins are being pulled into those frameworks, but unevenly and jurisdiction by jurisdiction.

So: they can feel similar from “I hold a digital dollar‑like thing” at the edge, but the power structure and legal footing underneath are very different.

What’s already happening
In the existing system, private banks already create most of the money in circulation by making loans; legal and policy analysis describes them as regulated franchises of the monetary sovereign.

In the newer system, stablecoin issuers are private entities creating digital dollar claims, with regulators increasingly treating them as part of the broader dollar infrastructure rather than as a totally separate universe.

Some policy commentary argues that governments see both stablecoins and CBDCs as tools to manage debt and create demand for their liabilities, with stablecoins being a more indirect, outsourced approach and CBDCs a direct, centralized one.

So yes: in practice, you already have state‑tolerated, state‑supervised private “digital dollar factories” running on public chains, and in many jurisdictions the state absolutely can pressure them (or order them) to freeze accounts, blacklist addresses, or change behavior.

Why that still isn’t a CBDC
The key difference is where the default choke point lives:

A stablecoin is a private liability. The government influences it through regulation, licenses, and enforcement, and can often get what it wants—but it is still pushing on a private intermediary.

A CBDC is a direct central bank liability with a ledger the state controls. Policy and technical analysis emphasize that this architecture gives the state real‑time visibility, direct control over balances, and built‑in programmability (limits, expiration, automatic taxation) in a way that has no historical parallel.

One commentator put it bluntly: stablecoins and CBDCs both enable control and censorship, but only CBDCs give the government direct access to your account by default; stablecoins still require going through the issuer or the banking rails that support that issuer.

So: some stablecoins are “CBDC‑like” in outcome (you can be frozen, monitored, and de‑banked), but they’re not identical in structure, which matters for how much friction there is when someone in power wants something done.
